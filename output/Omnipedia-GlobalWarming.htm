Global Warming

Global Warming or Climate Change refers to an observed upward
trend in the average global temperature year-on-year that has
been observed since scientific records started being kept in 1880.

While deep-core samples of water ice at the poles shows that the
Earth has experienced many cycles of warming and cooling over
the millennia, the upward trends observed since the 1950's
appears to be unique in the history of the planet.

While identifying the causes of the rise in global temperatures has
been contentious, the measurements speak for themselves; global
mean temperatures in 2064 are nearly 2.5 degrees Centigrade
higher than they were in 1900.

Numerous factors impact the warming or cooling of the planet,
including the composition of the atmosphere, solar luminosity or
brightness, and the change of the Earth's orbit around the sun.

The present trend due to the change in the orbit of Earth is a
cooling one. Models suggest that in 50,000 years we may enter a
global ice age due to the slight wandering of our solar orbit.

Changes in the composition of our atmosphere can lead to a
‘Greenhouse Effect'. First theorized in 1824, and verified
scientifically in 1860, the Greenhouse Effect is the process by
which the Earth's surface is warmed by radiation of heat from its
atmosphere, which is itself warmed by infra-red radiation from
the sun, as well as by heat reflecting back from the surface of the
Earth itself.

This reflected portion is reabsorbed, rather than escaping into
space, leading to the gradual heating of the surface.

The main Greenhouse Gasses that contribute to this trend are
water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane and ozone.

While the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has a
natural annually varying cycle, the atmospheric concentration
measured in 2065 was 700 parts per million (ppm), compared to
the 150 ppm measured a century earlier in 1965.

Carbon dioxide is released into the atmosphere by a variety of
natural as well as man-made sources.

Natural sources include atmospheric exchange from the ocean,
plant and animal respiration, soil respiration as well as a
negligible quantity from volcanic eruptions.

Man-made sources include the use of fossil fuels, changes in land
usage and industrial processes, with the burning of fossil fuels
being by far and away the largest single man-made contributor.

Deforestation, both by man and by climate-change induced
drought or flooding has led to a substantial decrease in the
amount of carbon dioxide being naturally reabsorbed by the
carbon cycle.

Global Warming is responsible for a wide spectrum of observable
effects; glacial ice retreats further with each passing year, with
satellite observations predicting ice-free arctic summers by the
start of the 22nd century.

Extreme weather events, such as heat and cold waves are
becoming more common as long established high-altitude
atmospheric air currents change their paths, allowing arctic air to
penetrate further south than ever before.

Category 5 tropical storms form more readily in our present high
carbon dioxide, high temperature environment than at any earlier
point since records started being kept.

Sea levels continue to rise; currently up 18 inches on their average
height at the turn of the 21st century. This is partially due to the
melting of the ice-caps, but also because of the expansion of sea
water due to its increased average temperature.

Higher intensity storms and sea levels are leading to an increased
threat to coastal areas. Super-storms hit Singapore for the first
time in 2058, bringing with them enormous surge-tides which
caused millions of dollars' worth of damage to the nation, and
have been an annual feature since.

Low lying Pacific island nations are particularly at threat. The
Marshall Islands were washed away by an 8 meter storm surge
just hours after their complete evacuation was concluded in 2059.

The damage inflicted was so severe that a number of the smaller,
outlying islands simply vanished from satellite imagery.

No resettlement has been attempted.

In 2060, the Cook Islands were permanently evacuated as a
precautionary measure.

Changing weather patterns have led to far less annual rainfall
across most of Northern Africa, and driven by high winds, the
Sahara is encroaching at an alarming annual rate.

The majority of Egypt's population is now clustered around the
banks of the Nile, itself reduced to a relative trickle by the dam
projects of the Nile River Coalition (NRC) nations.

The rising sea presents yet another challenge; as its level increases
it starts to contaminate the fresh groundwater in coastal regions,
leading to even less of that most vital of resources, clean drinking
water.
