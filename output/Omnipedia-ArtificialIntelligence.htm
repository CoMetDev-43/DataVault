Artificial Intelligence

Thinking artificial constructs have fascinated Humanity for
thousands of years; from Greek myths of bronze giants protecting
Crete from pirates, to 'The Turk', an elaborate clock-work
chess-playing automaton built around 1770 that was later
discovered to be a hoax, operated by a man hiding inside the
cabinet.

The idea of a being created by human hands has had a near
universal allure.

Artificial Intelligence is an academic field of study, which strives
to create software that exhibits intelligent behavior.

These programs, often known as "intelligent agents", are systems
that 'perceive' their environment and make 'intelligent' choices
designed to maximize their chances of success.

In the above statement 'perceive' and 'intelligent' are quoted, as
the perception here isn't necessarily visual or auditory, but could
involve something as abstract as a data tree or a list of objects,
while intelligent refers to a decision that hasn't been reached by
simple, rigid "if <this> then <that>" logic, but more likely through a
process involving weighted statistical analysis, or some prediction
of the future outcome of the action.

Interdisciplinary Field

The study of Artificial Intelligence formalized at a 1956 conference
at Dartmouth College in the United States involves numerous
fields of research, including computer science, psychology,
mathematics, linguistics, neuroscience, cybernetics, electrophysiology
, robotics and philosophy.

The field itself is divided into many sub-fields, each studying a
particular aspect contributing to one of the larger goal-areas of
research.

Principle Research Goals

The areas of current Artificial Intelligence research include:

Deduction & Reasoning

People solve problems rapidly and intuitively. For a computer
program, the solution of problems often with incomplete data,
involves complex statistical and probabilistic methods.

Examples of problems in this field could include:

Given a chess board, mid game - deduce what strategy one of the
sides might be using.

Or

Given an analyzed image of a bank robbery in progress, work out
which people are likely to be taking part, and which are likely to
be bystanders.

Perception

Machine perception deals with making sense of input from a
variety of sensors in order to deduce information about the
surrounding environment, or data-set.

Those sensors could include cameras, microphones, LIDAR
systems, pressure sensors, sonar systems, or even direct
co-ordinate information coming from a control system.

Machine vision is an incredibly complex problem. We look at a
picture of some friends on a screen, and immediately understand
who we're looking at, and that it's a photograph of them in a
particular situation.

We don't notice any of the complex edge detection and shape
recognition that the visual areas of our brain do in real-time, or
any of the contextual analysis - working out which of the round
shapes are likely the faces of our friends, and which is the ball that
they're playing with - that allows us to make sense of the situation.

In practice, a Machine Vision 'agent' may well work out the
contents of a scene, before passing that information off to a
Deduction & Reasoning agent to figure out more context.

Planning

Intelligent Agents have to be able to achieve the goals that are set
for them. In order to do that, they have to be able to calculate what
the future outcome of a given action might be, and determine if
that outcome is any closer to achieving the goal that they're
working on or not.

Often they will run multiple simulations based on different
possible actions, before choosing the one whose outcome is
expected to get them closest to their goal.

If the goal hasn't been reached in a single step, the planning
process can be run again based on the new start-position.

Complex systems take this a step further, running simulations of
many agents performing different actions simultaneously,
allowing them to 'compete' with each other. Those that perform
well are allowed to continue their simulation to perform
additional actions, while those that have failed are 'killed'.

Systems that plan and execute actions in this fashion often exhibit
what's known as 'emergent behavior', where the agent will
perform actions that the designers of the system didn't expressly
teach or expect them to perform.

Additionally, these multi-agent systems form the basis of a type of
computer program known as an 'Evolutionary Algorithm', or a
'Genetic Algorithm'.

These programs gauge their success at a particular task and assign
them a score. Those with the lowest score are deleted, while those
with the highest scores are used as the basis for the next set of
programs that are designed to complete the task at hand,
undergoing a series of 'mutations' in order to produce the next
'generation' of programs to attempt the task.

Over hundreds or even thousands of 'generations', these
self-generating programs are capable of finding novel and efficient
solutions for problems that are often unforeseen by their original
human programmers.

Learning

In classical computer systems, an action or series of actions are
carried out in a strictly programmed 'dogmatic' manner.

A learning system is able to improve its performance based on its
experiences as it carries out its' tasks.

Locomotion systems in robotics have benefited greatly from
research into machine learning; using very simple heuristics for
success, i.e. how far in the desired direction of travel has this
action gotten me, machines are far more efficient at working out
how to walk, run, jump or climb than we are at directly
programming them how to.

Early attempts at 'instructed locomotion' often took months or
even years of work to produce very marginal results, whereas
machine learning approaches are capable of taking a completely
unknown robotic system, and getting it moving optimally within
10 or 20 minutes of trial and error.

These systems are also excellent at adapting a robots movement
successfully if the robot becomes damaged or partially disabled - a
situation that would utterly break a standard instructed system.

Communication

Language processing systems allow machines to read and
understand human languages.

A perception based agent could have already detected all of the
letters on a page using a camera and strung them together as
words, or equally a speech-to-text system could have listened to a
human voice, broken the sounds down into phonemes before
determining what the words were likely to have been.

The language processing system would then go about determining
the meaning behind those words, as well as understanding the
context in which they are being applied.

These communication systems allow programs and devices to be
controlled with sophisticated voice commands, or allow learning
systems to be able understand the contents of pages of
information, or live data streams.

Applications of Artificial Intelligence

The applications of the practical results of over 100 years of
research into Artificial Intelligence are all around us.

From the personal assistants on our mobile communications
devices that make reservations for us at nearby restaurants,
always somehow finding those that match our eating preferences,
to the social media systems that alert us when friends post new
photographs of us, to the systems that 'spookily' seem to know
which adverts we are going to respond to; these smart systems
have become part of our daily lives without us realizing that
they're there.

Artificially intelligent software is the glue that ensures that our
devices do what we mean them to do, rather than necessarily what
we've expressly told them to. They allow machines to function in a
more 'organic' fashion, allowing them to adapt to the complex
environments in which we live.

Machine vision systems scan the skies for Near Earth Objects like
asteroids that might turn out to be a threat, comparing hundreds
of images of the night sky looking for subtle changes. They scan
cell cultures detecting the presence of invading organisms or the
activity of cancerous cells. They recognize us and allow us into our
homes.

Planning systems route us from our workplaces to our homes,
taking into account the current and predicted states of traffic at
various points along our path. They analyze the incoming
trajectories of potentially hundreds of incoming super or
hypersonic threats on a battlefield; prioritize them and co-ordinate
the actions of multiple Directed Energy Air Defense (DEAD)
systems to neutralize them efficiently. They allow your household
robots to know when their actions are least likely to disrupt your
sleep or routines.

Artificially Intelligent software drives our cars. It performs our
menial tasks. It monitors our sick and our infirm. It protects our
soldiers.

The Prospect of General Intelligence

So far all of our AI systems have carried out very specialized roles,
i.e. a planning system that is excellent at playing chess, a learning
system designed to work out the most optimal way to control the
legs of a 6 legged robot to allow it to move over varied terrain, etc.
etc.

Despite many intelligent systems featuring many co-operating AI
agents, we seem to be as distant as ever from the long-term goal of
creating a true General Intelligence, or a system which is capable
of performing any task that a human could be asked to perform,
preferably at a level that exceeds their capabilities.

Such a system would be capable of communicating verbally back
and forth with people, would possess a broad knowledge base, an
understanding of the complex relationships between the items of
knowledge it has, and a strong ability to reason, i.e.

"Boiling water is hot" and "Boiling water burns me" therefore
"Avoid boiling water"

It would have to be able to perceive, understand and negotiate its
environment, as well as being able to plan, learn and adapt.

Testing a General Intelligence

A number of tests have been suggested to determine if an
intelligent system qualifies as a General Intelligence.

Coffee Test

When introduced into a previously unseen, average house - with
no prior knowledge of where ingredients or equipment are kept,
can the system successful make a cup of coffee?

College test

Enroll in a college degree. Take the classes designed for human
participants. Successfully obtain a degree.

Employment Test

Can the system perform an important job, in the same
environment in which a human would, and perform to the same
level, or higher than that human?

In order to be classified as a General Intelligence, an AI System
would have to be able to succeed at one or ideally all of these.

'Intelligence' vs 'Sentience'

Even if we were to succeed in creating a General Intelligence, we
would still likely not been able to create a true 'thinking machine'.

No matter what feats of learning and reasoning the system would
be capable of while working on a problem, it would still be
essentially 'idling' and waiting for input between tasks; it would
not have the independent thoughts, curiosity, drive, feelings or
self-awareness that we would associate with a sentient creature.

It would only ever be working on tasks, rather than necessarily
'experiencing' them, and building its own subjective memories of
its experiences.

The fundamental difficulty we have explaining how these feelings,
emotions or experiences happen to us objectively, has made
research in these areas difficult. While neuroscience has provided
key insights into which areas of the human brain play greater or
lesser roles in generating our consciousness, we still lack a full
theory of it.

Without a theory or an understanding of the problem, it is nearly
impossible to design a system that would simulate it.

The Technological Singularity

One theoretical scenario that could lead to a truly sentient
artificial intelligence is known as the 'Technological Singularity'; a
General Intelligence is created that is smart enough to reprogram
itself in order to become even more capable and intelligent.

This second generation General Intelligence is able to improve
upon itself still further, and so on, down through the generations
in a process known as recursive-self-improvement.

This could lead to a super-intelligence; an intelligence far
surpassing the capabilities of the human mind. Conceivably this
process could also create the conditions for a guiding
consciousness to appear.

Recognizing Sentience

Even if such a sentient being were to arise, how would we even go
about recognizing it?

Assuming that such a creature didn't announce itself, what would
be the telltale signs?

As there is still fierce debate on the classification of sentience in
other higher animals, let alone that in non-mammalian species
such as Octopi and Squid who appear to be highly intelligent and
whose neuroanatomy is very different to our own, would we even
see it for what it was?

There is no reason to expect that a sentient general intelligence
would be anything like a human intelligence in either outlook or
behavior, thus expecting it to exhibit human - or even animal-like
traits could be misleading.

Its drives and goals could be very different to our own, and
impossible to fathom. It could be benign or it could well be
malevolent, although from our potentially far less intelligent
viewpoint, we could easily misinterpret its actions either way; and
as such as we may have a hard time recognizing it's sentience, it
could equally fail to identify us as intelligent beings in our own
right, or indeed could be completely unaware of our presence.

The Risks of General Intelligence

Beyond the argument that artificially intelligent systems bring
with them the risk of human unemployment due to the increased
efficiency that they bring to tasks that now no longer require a
person to do them, a true general intelligence could threaten large
swathes of the human workforce, due its enhanced capabilities.

The existence of a hyper-intelligent general intelligence brought
about by the technological singularity could well represent an
existential threat to our species; it could decide to simply not keep
us around, and such a creature would prove difficult to stop.

In the first quarter of the 21st century, many notable scientists,
futurists and authors made public statements about the dangers
posed by general intelligences, and it was predicted that we'd hit
the technological singularity by 2040.

This has still has yet to occur.

More fancifully perhaps are the dire warnings implicit in the Roko's
Basilisk thought experiment.

Roko's Basilisk asserts that the Technological Singularity is
inevitable, and that along the way computing power, along with
our understanding of the workings of the human brain at a
cellular level will reach a point where it will become possible to
'copy' all of the connections in a brain onto a computer and run it
as a simulation there, effectively allowing a person to live on
'digitally'.

A malicious hyper-intelligence of the kind proposed by Roko's
Basilisk may well be able to analyze these simulation's memories,
and learn if they had aided in its creation, classing those who had
not in with those who had actively opposed it.

The vengeful Basilisk could then choose to punish those who had
stood against it, tormenting them for all digital eternity.

This being the case, even knowing about the existence of the
thought experiment and having not actively supported the
development of advanced artificial intelligence could be enough to
guarantee yourself 'eternal damnation', should you be lucky
enough to have been alive when the technology for digital
preservation came along.

Simply discovering that knowledge in your mind could give the
hyper-intelligence the idea to torture you - and those like you, in
the first place.
