DO "ALTERED" HUMANS BELONG IN PRO SPORTS?

By Lincoln "Buzz" Bratton

Whether its carbon fiber prosthetics or the hot new cybernetic enhancement
technology rumored to be right around the corner, there is quiet rumble in
the sports world building to what will inevitably be a serious debate come
the 2076 Summer Olympic Games in Brisbane, Australia.

Should we or shouldn't we continue to allow humans with altered human
forms compete at the highest level of sports? Would a Quarterback who can
throw a tight spiral 75 yards with a simple flick of his cybernetic wrist be a
fair opponent for those non-enhanced players competing against him?

Does a non-altered human even have a chance against one with enhanced
capabilities? Are professional sports going to change forever? Those are just
some of the questions being tossed around the sports world these days and
there is no shortage of opinions.

"They should have their own leagues. Look, I'm not saying they shouldn't play
sports. I'm just saying they shouldn't play in our league," said Brock Landree,
Quarterback of Pro Football's Toronto Golden Monarchs.

Landree's comments seem to echo the same sentiment of those
professional athletes who have spoken out on the subject. The majority of
who fear that a move towards advanced body modification will create a
culture of athletes, who will replace perfectly functioning limbs with those
that give them a competitive advantage or worse yet, put "real" athletes out
of work completely.

"Why wouldn't you? If it's the difference between a $1.2M contract and riding
the bench for a season versus a $45M contract and being in the starting
five... c'mon it's a no brainer," said Jayson Hitchcock, starting point guard for
the San Diego Moon Sharks.

"I don't like it. Feels like the steroid debate twenty, thirty years ago. Any
records broken by athletes with artificial limbs should have an asterisk. I was
blessed with this body, I wasn't built in a lab," said Jo Eduardo starting libero
for the Sao Paolo Tropics of the MLBVL.

Other athletes, like Carmichael Toab of the Marseille Monsieurs FC, believe
it's just the natural evolution of sports. His controversial comments last
month after the Monsieur's victory over the Lisbon Imperials in the IEFA
semi-finals stirred up a frenzy in the European media.

"This debate seems childish. People said the same thing when the barriers of
race were challenged in sporting. This is just the next evolution. I am not
afraid of anything. My skill is unsurpassed, whether I am human or machine.
No one can stop me either way," said Toab.

Toab, of course, was also one of the first athletes to suggest that he would
undergo enhancement surgery if the IEFA were to pass the pending
modification rules currently under review with league President Jacque
Courtemanche.

Fans and owners also have weighed in on the debate.

43% of fans polled by Current Events Aggregate Sports last month said they
would be "somewhat interested" in seeing their favorite non-enhanced
athletes take on their enhanced counterparts. That's up nearly 16% from
last year's poll.

"I don't have a horse in this race. I'm an entertainer at heart, so if the
commissioner and the rules committee think this is a good move for the
league and the fans agree, I'm game," said owner Lamar "Tex" Hood of the
Waco Wildcatters.

Although most enhanced athletes operate on the fringes of the sports world
today, as more professional sports leagues adopt policies that are
accepting of these unique athletes the debate will rage on - do they belong?
