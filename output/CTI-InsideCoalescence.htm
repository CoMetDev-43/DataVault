INSIDE COALESCENCE

Author: Rich Tracy

Date: July 2058

Walking up the steps to the Coalescence Corporation's headquarters in Zurich is striking;
the huge conical one hundred and thirty story building dominates the Zurich skyline.
Stepping inside the lobby visitors are welcomed by a large marble C logo that overlooks
the room. The bustle of workers moving past the security checkpoints underscore the
rapid growth this company has seen over the last ten years.

The Coalescence Corporation stands on the verge of a technological breakthrough that
could transform the fundamental relationship we have with technology. Already, it is
universally recognized as the world leader in robotics. The company handles around 90%
of government robotic contracts in the world. Their technology is ubiquitous, seen in every
car currently manufactured as well as in the latest in military exo-skeletons. They are
currently the largest human prosthetics manufacturer in the world, and with their new
breakthrough they want to become everyone's constant companion.

That breakthrough is their Direct Neural Interface, or DNI.

The rapid evolution of human enhancement technology has brought new opportunities to
a business already generating annual revenue in excess of $50bn (£33.7bn). The
announcement of DNI going into trials sent Coalescence Corporation stock (NYSE: CLSCA)
skyrocketing on the trading floor early in June. We're told that DNI will fundamentally
change the way we interact with the technology all around us. I am here along with others
in the media to see it in action for the first time in public. After a brief tour the
Coalescence Corporation Director of Public Relations, Peter Pfeiffer escorted us into a
meeting room on the thirty second floor. Shortly after we take our seats the lights dim
and Peter Pfeiffer begins a presentation taking us through the history of human input
devices, taking us from punch cards to air gestures. "Ninety years ago Douglas Engelbart
at The Mother of All Demos introduced the public to the mouse, setting the stage for a
world changing technological shift that started the computing revolution. Today we
introduce you to DNI."

Pfeifer moves to center stage and begins the tell us where this technology was born, "It
began, just 15 years ago, as an experiment to enhance the capabilities of prosthetics,
enabling superior motor control as well as advanced haptic feedback. Things moved
along quickly, by connecting the input and output of what essentially is a robotic limb
directly to the human nervous system, Coalescence made the first steps into what now is
a Direct Neural Interface with a computer. A person with such an interface can interact
with technology in ways never before thought possible."

Standing up from the front row, a man in his late twenties joins Pfeifer onstage; He is
introduced as Travis Buehler. "Travis lost both of his arms in a motorcycle accident ten
years ago. The Direct Neural Interface has not only given him two fully functional hands
and arms, it has allowed him to control every aspect of this presentation today while he
was sitting in the front row." Pfeiffer goes on to explain that with the DNI Travis can
monitor and control a vast array of technology, what took a team of men in the past is
now being done by one, with zero need for physical interaction.

"Because of the direct link the problems associated with augmented and enhanced
reality disappear. The user is never overwhelmed with data; the data he wants is just
there." The large screen behind them switches to a feed from one of Travis' eyes. "He has
instant access to unlimited amounts of data." We see on the screen the flow of data that
Travis is coordinating through his DNI, it's remarkable to witness, as Travis stands on
stage telling us about the coordination of the presentation, we see him controlling the
cameras, lights and video feeds. He turns, and the camera feed behind him switches to a
close up on the back of his head revealing the DNI.

My first impression is that this revolutionary device looks remarkably unobtrusive. The
only thing noticeable on the man standing on stage is a small metal port located on the
back of his neck. Pfeifer tells us that this is an early prototype and in the future there
would be no outward indication of the DNI.

We are then shown a promotional video, highlighting some of uses for the DNI; it shows a
doctor controlling multiple medical robots to perform faster and less invasive surgeries.
There is a scene of a firefighters moving into a burning building, as they receive live
updates on the buildings structure that they see overlaid onto the world around them.
Soldiers on a battlefield have biometric data sent to their commander as they move
through a street and targets are outlined and updated.

Pfeifer steps back onto the stage, "The Direct Neural Interface entered its final round of
trials last month. We will continue to innovate and improve on its capabilities and design
though this process. We will have DNI in hands of professionals before the end of the
year, and plans to widen the distribution to the public with the next two years. What I find
most thrilling is that DNI is not only first in class, it is creating the class. As with any
revolutionary innovation I am sure the most exciting things possible with DNI are yet to be
dreamt of."

As I walk back onto the streets of Zurich with my fellow journalists, I can't help but
wonder, did I just witness the Mother of all Demos for this century?
