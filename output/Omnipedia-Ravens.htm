Ravens

Ravens make up the largest members of the of the Corvus genus
of birds, which also includes crows, rooks and jackdaws.

They are large, omnivorous birds with gently curving beaks that
are predominantly black in color, sometimes sporting white or
grey plumage. Unlike some species of birds, there is little
difference in size, shape or coloration between the genders.

In the wild, Ravens live for between 20-30 years, however the
eldest Raven kept in captivity reportedly survived to 59 years of
age.

Ravens are found throughout the entire Northern Hemisphere, as
well as in North Africa, South-Central USA, Central America and
the Middle East. Of all of the members of the family, the Common
Raven ( Corvus Corax ) enjoys the widest range.

Ravens exhibit many examples of intelligent behavior, and score
highly on tests designed by scientists to measure animal
intelligence. In the wild they have been observed bait-fishing;
using crumbs of scavenged bread to attract fish, as well as crafting
tools from leaves, grass stems and twigs in order to extract insect
grubs from inside rotting logs.

They have also been seen to hide food in caches for later retrieval
during winters when foraging is scarce. This kind of behavior
suggests foresight, planning and 'episodic memory' and is
considered to be quite sophisticated.

Behaviorists have recorded many instances of Ravens engaging in
play; another behavior typically only found in highly intelligent
animals - young Common Ravens have been seen repeatedly
sliding down snow covered slopes. The animal's nidopallium ; the
region of the birds brain associated with cognitive tasks has been
found to be broadly the same relative size as the functionally
equivalent part of the brain found in humans and chimpanzees.

Long before modern science began to confirm it, Ravens have
always been seen as intelligent creatures, as demonstrated by the

myths and legends of many cultures that surround them.
