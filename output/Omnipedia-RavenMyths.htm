Raven Myths

The ubiquitous and wide spread Common Raven with its sinister
dark coloring and distinctive cry has captured the imagination of
mankind throughout the ages, appearing in the myths and
legends of many regions and cultures.

To the ancient Greeks and Romans, Ravens were associated with
prophecy and good fortune and were thought to be the
messengers of the god Apollo.

In the stories of the Irish Celtic hero Cu Chulainn (whose father,
the god Lugh's name is derived from the word for ' Raven '), it is not
until the goddess Morrigan alights on his shoulder in the form of a
Raven that his enemies finally believe that he has actually died.

The saga, the Landnamabok - or ' book of settlements ' written
around 1000 AD credits a Raven with guiding the Viking
Hrafna-Floki's ships to settle on what would become known as
Iceland.

To the Vikings and early Germanic peoples, Ravens were strongly
associated with the deity Odin who presided over the afterlife and
was seen as having dominion over war, death and knowledge. His
Ravens Huginn and Muninn (Thought and Memory) would fly
over the Earth each day, before reporting back to him.

Swedish folklore holds that the cries of Ravens heard in marshes
and on moors are actually the calls of the ghosts of murder victims
whose bodies have been left undiscovered in those remote places.

English folklore holds that if the Ravens leave the Tower of
London, then the Kingdom is sure to fall.

Meanwhile many of the indigenous peoples of the North West
coast of the Americas have folklore that casts the bird as a trickster
god, who was also responsible for the creation of the world.

It is likely the animals' carrion diet, and thus its association with
battlefields and places of the dead has meant that it has ended up
being commonly linked to the transition between life and death.
