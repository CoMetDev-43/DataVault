Rare Earth Elements

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC)
defines a Rare Earth Element as an element that is either a
member of the lanthanide group as well as scandium and yttrium ;
for a total of 17 elements from the periodic table.

Rare earth's are actually relatively plentiful, with cerium being
about as abundant as copper; however unlike the non-rare earth
elements, they are rarely found in concentrated easily mined
seams or deposits of minerals, instead being diffusely distributed.
This is the property that leads to their scarcity.

Uses

Rare earths are used in small quantities in many areas of
high-tech industry and manufacturing; from the solid state lasers
used in long distance data communication and air defense
applications, to the permanent magnets used in medical imaging
and high powered, light weight electric motors used in a wide
variety of applications including robotics and electric vehicles.

Additionally, the lanthanides are used widely as catalysts in
petroleum refinery, as well as in other large-scale commercial
processes.

Solid state lasers are not the only military application of rare
earths. Lanthanides may be found in components for low-light
imaging equipment, targeting radar and propulsion systems.

Their reactivity to very specific wavelengths of light sees them
used in medical diagnostic applications, as well as in portable
devices used to 'sniff' for toxins, contaminants and explosives; the
later in security applications.

Global Production

China has been historically the largest producer of rare earth
elements, producing roughly twice as much as the second largest
producer, the USA since the late 1990s.

The rest of the World's production, totaling roughly half as much
again as the USA, comes from Australia, Brazil, Canada,
Greenland, Kenya, North Korea, South Africa, Sweden (where the
first rare earths were discovered in 1787), Tanzania and Vietnam.

The extent of the discoveries of niobium and neodymium in
Kenya in 2039 are still being explored, however conservative
estimates project that Kenya will overtake the US' production of rare
earths before 2075.

Origin

Rare earth elements are heavier than iron (with the exception of

scandium ) and are thought to be produced when either white
dwarf or supergiant stars exhaust their fuel, and collapse inwards
before exploding in catastrophic supernovae explosions, in which
lighter elements are fused into heavier ones.
