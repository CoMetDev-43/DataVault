Ballistic Missile Defense

BMD (Ballistic Missile Defense) refers to any weapon system, or
group of systems that are designed to protect an individual or area
from ballistic threats; including flat-trajectory projectiles like
bullets or RPGs, indirect fire weapons like mortars or short,
medium and long range missiles and ICBMs.

Effective Ballistic Defense Systems were first deployed in the early
1990's, being used to protect civilian population centers in the
Middle East from medium range missiles. Those systems, like the
majority of those that followed employed multiple batteries of
interceptor missiles and a distributed network of targeting radar
systems.

The radar systems track incoming threats and determine which
are going to pose a threat to valuable assets, be they military or
civilian.

In response, targeting information is rapidly computed and one or
more interceptors are launched.

The interceptor vehicles operate by either hitting the incoming
warheads directly (Kinetic Hit-to-kill) requiring great accuracy, or
by detonating in the path of the incoming missile creating a
shrapnel field likely to disrupt the target.

The early systems were expensive (upwards of 800,000 US Dollars
per shot) and error prone (failure to launch, failure to intercept).

All systems based on missiles or rockets are also susceptible to
saturation due to salvoes of less expensive attack missiles being
launched.

The early 20th century saw multiple parties developing their own
'theatre wide' defense systems, where distributed radar and
launch systems (such as Aegis cruisers ) designed to attack threats
during their different phases (launch, boost-phase, mid-course and
re-entry) of their flights.

There are also far more 'localized' versions of these systems
available, from ship based systems that protect their mother
vessel from attack by sea-skimming missiles by setting up cross
fire zones of fire using high-speed multi-barrel machine guns for
the missile to fly through, to anti-sniper systems mounted on
vehicles that employ radar to determine the origin point of an
incoming shot while it's in flight, before returning fire on that
point with high-explosives using a rapid-gimbaling 50 caliber rifle.

In the late 2020's the projectiles used by these defensive systems
were starting to be phased out in favor of kilowatt class solid-state
laser based systems, whose development had been hastened by
the Allied Drone Defense act of 2025.

Laser based systems are much cheaper to operate (hundreds of
dollars per shot, vs. hundreds of thousands), are far more
mechanically reliable and don't suffer the limited ammunition
problems experienced by missile based interceptor systems.

The kilowatt class systems however required relatively long
'time-on-target' tracking in order to do enough damage to the
threat to render it inoperable, and were defeated by smarter
navigation systems that would execute rapid, unpredictable 'jinks'
in the trajectory of the missile or warhead, and by the
introduction of hypersonic munitions; missiles that arrive at their
target so quickly that you either simply can't keep the laser on
them, or can't keep the laser on them long enough to do enough
damage.

In the mid 2040's mega-watt class solid-state lasers became
available and along with advanced artificially intelligent target
prioritization systems, form the basis for the effective Directed
Energy Air Defense (DEAD) systems that we see in service today.
