Local Manufacture

3D printing technologies advanced rapidly over the first quarter of
the 21st century, becoming increasingly versatile.

The ability to incorporate multiple materials such as metals and
ceramics into a single 'construction run', as well as the capability
to print electronic circuits directly into finished products have
allowed significantly more sophisticated and varied items to be
manufactured.

As subtractive printing methods were added, it became possible to
move away from printing individual components, and onto
printing complete, working machines.

For the first time since the Industrial Revolution of the 19th
Century, the entire production line was compressed down into a
singular unit, requiring little or no Human intervention to
operate.

Large scale specialized factories containing complex production
lines were converted, becoming 'general manufactories' capable of
producing a bewildering variety of complex, consumer ready
goods.

With the global mass-transit of goods and components becoming
increasingly expensive due to rising fuel prices, the World's
largest economies switched to the local manufacture of products
and components at home rather, than relying on what had been
historically cheaper manufacturers abroad.

Over a 10 year period between 2030 and 2040, the global freight
industry contracted by an unprecedented 60 percent, triggering a
series of recessions in the developing nations, whose export
focused economies lacked strong internal consumption based
markets of their own.

Global trade hubs such as Singapore were hit especially hard by
the fall in global shipping, suffering mass unemployment as many
freight facilities fell into disuse and disrepair.

This would become known as the ' recession of the poorest ', and its
effects are still being felt 30 years later.
