Fuel Cells

A Fuel Cell is a device that reacts positively charged hydrogen ions
with either oxygen or an oxidizing agent to produce electricity.

The byproduct of the reaction is water vapor.

Most fuel cells in use today use a polymer electrolyte membrane
(PEM), which allows protons (hydrogen ions) to pass through it,
but restricts the passage of electrons, which would otherwise
short-out the fuel cell.

PEM based fuel cells scale well, making them as useful for
powering small portable devices as they are for powering vehicles.

Hydrogen is stored safely in a carbohydrate, mitigating the risk of
explosion or leak and is released by the addition of a catalyzing
enzyme.

Carbohydrates are produced cheaply on an industrial scale in
bioreactors using genetically engineered algae, which use
photosynthesis to convert sunlight into starch.

In situations where very high power is required, a graphene based
super-capacitor may be more appropriate, but due to the
continuous production of power achieved by fuel cells, as long as
fuel is available, fuel cells offer superior endurance.
